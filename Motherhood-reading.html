Graphic Novel: Motherhood in Crisis

500 word reflection

The choice to portray these intimate stories of motherhood through interactive graphic novels make the stories visually explicit and memorable in a way that would be difficult to be achieved through text alone. Another element that I found particularly effective was the intersplicing of real images into the comics. The difference between the two mediums isn’t as much jarring as it is a constant reminder of the reality behind the stories. The real images begin filtering through slowly as each graphic novel draws to a close and begins narrating the futures of their drawn protagonists. There was one page in Heela’s story that intercut the real woman with a graphically depicted child—the blending of the two mediums felt peaceful in communicating the thin barrier between reality and fiction while still protecting the identities of the children depicted online.
In the first chapter, featuring Memunatu’s story, the positioning of the characters during birth forces the viewer into an uncomfortable spectator role: unable to help and subject to the same horrors as the protagonist. The graphic novel is able to fully immerse us into the horrific realities these women go through without placing them in compromising positions that would risk retriggering their trauma. The illustrations of the school children in Heela’s story made it more heartbreaking since it realistically displays children facing these horrific circumstances. Specifically, the lack of healthcare and sex-education resulting in the dangerous self-inflicted abortions of children in Sierra Leone.
To me, Kaidu’s story had hit the hardest as it showcased how multiple systemic issues can overlap to devastating effects. Her struggles not only stemmed from the underfunded healthcare and education system but her grief is maximized by gendered discrimination and the Ebola & COVID epidemics. Each step forward felt like a miracle. The real photos of herself and her healthy children reflected the light at the end of the tunnel. Yet, the graphic novel remains tethered to reality by ending on a black screen with white text proclaiming the closing, then reopening, of the hospital and the return of an epidemic. The black text screen is what draws us into the world through set-in-reality contexts. In this instance it also becomes the tool that draws us out of the narrative back into the “everyday news” of reality.
In both Kaidu’s story and Tetteh’s story, the use of video interviews from nurses and social workers allow the graphic novel to shift fully into the documentary sphere. Their commentary is–for me–what places the story into the present moment. This graphic novel series does an excellent job of immersing the viewer into an intimate narrative while reminding us of the ever-prevailing systems of violence that still remain in our present. These are not merely stories of tragedy or perseverance that occurred in the distant past but stories actively being created in our present. I believe that the constant temporal reminders (e.g. set in the year 2020) is what is most depressing. Although modern systems exist to protect the rights of women, children, and their bodies, they are distinctly absent in Sierra Leone. As all good documentaries do, “Motherhood in Crisis” is a strong push to action for the viewer. The injustices are communicated without barriers and without happy endings.
