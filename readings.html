Susan Sontag's "In Plato's Cave"

500 word reflection

Susan Sontag, in her piece “In Plato’s Cave,” argues that photography is incapable of “narration” (24) in the same way that the written text and the moving image communicate information. I would agree that photography cannot completely invent an individual’s moral and political conscience, however it would be too limiting to state that photography can only be used to “goad conscience” (25). Our understanding of any text or imagery must first arise from a point of reference and its cognitive categorization, after all. On the other hand, I disagree with Sontag as I believe that still images are capable of narration and facilitating understanding through masterful composition, the subject matter, and contextualization of the scene. Sontag raises a good point in that photography tends to rely more on the pre-existing knowledge of the viewer than other mediums like novels or movies, however I argue that a still image can be comparable to a short poem. Though the image is only “a neat slice of time” (18), I believe its details are merely a condensed version of the typical narrative shown in movies and texts. In the same way that a poem or textbook relies on the reader’s knowledge of subject matter lexicon and concepts, a photograph relies on a similar process of contextualization. However, I believe that photographers not only replicate the reality through an observer’s lens, but actively craft the narrative through intentional framing and editing in post.

In my photography and illustration, I hope to not only capture the present but color each moment with a distinct emotional overlay. As Sontag points out, photographs often stir remembrance and reflection in the viewer. In capturing any human or animal subjects, I want to be able to capture and revise the memory of the subject in a way that paints a “reality” meaningful to me. While I have the compulsion to capture the moment to “re-live” it, I have a greater compulsion to capture the moment so that I may be able to share it with others. In the same way that stories or paintings can communicate experiences that verbal words fail to convey, I believe that photographs can convey the ‘feeling’ of a memory that text cannot. However, in doing so, I concur with Sontag that taking a photograph inherently objectifies the subject in the image as the photographer “lays claim to another reality” (16) that exists beyond the reality of the moment itself. There can be an interesting point made to explore what happens when a photographer takes a photo of herself—would she capture only the subjective image of her own reflection? One of my favorite art mediums is the comic, which combines illustration with text in a way that conveys a broader narrative. It is the blending of reality and fiction that attracts me to the genre of surrealism in photography. I hope to edit photomontages that capture the dreamlike quality of fuzzy, incomplete memories and their interaction with our present understanding of ourselves. As someone who often finds themselves lost in thought, I seek to find clarity in the surreal and fill in the gaps that clear-cut logic cannot.

Nesrine Malik, 'AI slop' distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster

500 word reflection

From the onset, Malik’s scathing tone towards our current digital landscape reads as both surprising and well-deserved. The sudden onslaught of AI-generated videos have polluted our feeds to the point where the line between reality and fiction is blurred. More than that, Malik argues that the use of AI-generated content is an act of conscious distortion of information from power-seeking groups. For instance, Malik points to the use of AI for right wing conservatism media campaigns. I find that Malik summarizes the appeal of rightwing AI-generated content very well in registering itself as a “nostalgic” and “triumphant” echo of the past. There is a poetic point to be argued in the fact that AI-generated content is inherently trained using media from the past which leans towards conservative ideas and representations.

I found it increasingly interesting that AI is referred to as the “new aesthetic of fascism” as much of its content simulates patriotic and traditional ideas. While we tend to associate fascism with Germany’s Hitler or Spain’s Franco, I believe that pre-World War II America also showcased qualities that bordered on fascism and extremist hate through discrimination. The U.S. has a splitting contrast in its historical media representation. America in newspaper advertisements, films, and novels all point to it as the land of the free–a soldier who fights against fascist enemies to provide freedom for all. However, those same advertisements, films, and white-authored novels also painted America as a deeply patriotic state whose interests are determined by the white-dominant majority. Occasions of institutional and extremist discrimination towards minorities, performed in the name of ‘protecting’ the white majority, border on fascist ideology. It comes in logical order that content created from AI trained on these past media sources would promote an image of fascist America.

Malik further points out the many dangers of AI-generated content when spread on platforms like WhatsApp. When unable to check the authenticity of a source, any user would be misled by what appears otherwise to be ‘real’ footage of an event. I believe that the consequential “densensitization” to extremist media is in actuality a defense mechanism that we learnt in order to lower the emotional toll that comes with every real or AI-generated content. Due to the current state of our journalistic news, I have heard many comparisons between the U.S. government and the totalitarian regime from Orwell’s 1984 novel. Most specifically, the quote, “The Party told you to reject the evidence of your eyes and ears. It was their final, most essential command,” has been applied to the numerous cases in which TV news have attempted to distort or cover up evidence of ICE brutality. The addition of AI-generated content, as Malik notes, makes it easier to fall prey to this line of thinking. When AI-content can no longer be distinguished from reality, what form of media can the public truly trust? My favorite quote out of the entire article is as follows: “You are immersed deeper and deeper into subjective worlds rather than objective reality.” I believe that the creation of ‘subjective worlds’ in social media algorithms tailored to our interests have not only impacted our consumption of news, but our relationship with our own thinking. In psychology, when someone ruminates too much in their own headspace, it can lead to extreme conclusions and irrational thinking. I believe that the increase of AI-content and AI-led algorithms will only exacerbate this issue.
