Susan Sontag's "In Plato's Cave"

500 word reflection

Susan Sontag, in her piece “In Plato’s Cave,” argues that photography is incapable of “narration” (24) in the same way that the written text and the moving image communicate information. I would agree that photography cannot completely invent an individual’s moral and political conscience, however it would be too limiting to state that photography can only be used to “goad conscience” (25). Our understanding of any text or imagery must first arise from a point of reference and its cognitive categorization, after all. On the other hand, I disagree with Sontag as I believe that still images are capable of narration and facilitating understanding through masterful composition, the subject matter, and contextualization of the scene. Sontag raises a good point in that photography tends to rely more on the pre-existing knowledge of the viewer than other mediums like novels or movies, however I argue that a still image can be comparable to a short poem. Though the image is only “a neat slice of time” (18), I believe its details are merely a condensed version of the typical narrative shown in movies and texts. In the same way that a poem or textbook relies on the reader’s knowledge of subject matter lexicon and concepts, a photograph relies on a similar process of contextualization. However, I believe that photographers not only replicate the reality through an observer’s lens, but actively craft the narrative through intentional framing and editing in post.

In my photography and illustration, I hope to not only capture the present but color each moment with a distinct emotional overlay. As Sontag points out, photographs often stir remembrance and reflection in the viewer. In capturing any human or animal subjects, I want to be able to capture and revise the memory of the subject in a way that paints a “reality” meaningful to me. While I have the compulsion to capture the moment to “re-live” it, I have a greater compulsion to capture the moment so that I may be able to share it with others. In the same way that stories or paintings can communicate experiences that verbal words fail to convey, I believe that photographs can convey the ‘feeling’ of a memory that text cannot. However, in doing so, I concur with Sontag that taking a photograph inherently objectifies the subject in the image as the photographer “lays claim to another reality” (16) that exists beyond the reality of the moment itself. There can be an interesting point made to explore what happens when a photographer takes a photo of herself—would she capture only the subjective image of her own reflection? One of my favorite art mediums is the comic, which combines illustration with text in a way that conveys a broader narrative. It is the blending of reality and fiction that attracts me to the genre of surrealism in photography. I hope to edit photomontages that capture the dreamlike quality of fuzzy, incomplete memories and their interaction with our present understanding of ourselves. As someone who often finds themselves lost in thought, I seek to find clarity in the surreal and fill in the gaps that clear-cut logic cannot.
